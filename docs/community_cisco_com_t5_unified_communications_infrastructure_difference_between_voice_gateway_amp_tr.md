The difference between a gateway and a trunk lies in the history of telephony. And by this I mean that these terms were in place before there was IP-based communications.
The way I've explained it to my students is that if the expectation that the target device will maintain the means of communication (analog to analog, digital to digital, IP to IP) then the connection to that device would be called a trunk. If the expectation that the target device would change the means of communication (analog to PRI, IP to PRI, IP to analog, etc.) then the connection to that device would be a gateway.
Remember that the original PBXs were analog hosting analog phones. The connection between two PBXs would be a Trunk because it is a connection carrying one call between the two systems, maintaining the analog means of communication on both the source system and the target system. Even if the trunk connection between the two PBXs was a T1 or E1, it would be a channelized T1 or E1 with analog channels. So, again, like-to-like and therefore still a trunk.
For a connection from an analog PBX to a PSTN-connected device, the PSTN-connected device would be assumed to have a PRI and so that PSTN-connected device (and its connection) would be considered a gateway. (Analog to PRI)
As telephony devices and systems involved, the underlying concept was maintained that a like-to-like expectation would have the connection be a trunk, and a like-to-different expectation would have the connection be a gateway.
The earliest widely used IP telephony protocol was H.323, and that was used to connect the analog PBX to a larger IP-based network, so an H.323 connection was considered a gateway. Once the technology existed such that H.323-based endpoints (especially early video) connected to H.323-based routers which reached out to other H.323 routers to eventually get a call to another H.323 video device, the term "gateway" for those initial connections stuck around, but you did hear the term "Trunk" for the inter-router H.323 connections.
This means that MGCP would be a gateway because it is IP phones on CUCM to an MGCP-managed PRI. For CUCM an H.323 connection was assumed to be a connection to a router-controlled PRI, so that was also a gateway.
If you think about CUCM intercluster trunks (the old kind, not SIP trunks) the expectation is that the source system and the target system would maintain the IP-based means of communication, so those are trunks.
SIP is modern IP-based communications and the expectation is that the target device will also be IP-based (and usually SIP) so that connection was named a trunk from the start. That is true whether it is a SIP intercluster trunk between two CUCM clusters, or a SIP trunk to an external system. SIP assumes IP-only even if the target does 'something else' on the other end, so SIP connections are all trunks.
Generically, a gateway is a device that connects systems that are not alike. Back in the days when token ring and Ethernet were interworked, the device connecting them was called a translational gateway. And if you think about the networking term "ip default gateway" you are connecting "inside" to "outside" and the term gateway makes sense.
To answer the original poster's question, as [@Jonathan Schulenberg](https://community.cisco.com/t5/user/viewprofilepage/user-id/324968) pointed out it depends on what you want to do. If you want CUCM to control the router and the FXO ports, then you would use MGCP. If you want CUCM to connect to the router, but have the router control the FXO ports then CUCM could either use an H.323 connection to the router or a SIP connection to the router. But SIP is recommended as it is more modern, while H.323 is being phased out of support, so you want to use SIP.
I hope you find this helpful. I know it was long-winded, but I find understanding the history behind a thing helps me remember it better and the context helps me apply it to new situations. Please let me know if you have any questions or if I can help with other information.

The difference between a gateway and a trunk lies in the history of telephony. And by this I mean that these terms were in place before there was IP-based communications.
The way I've explained it to my students is that if the expectation that the target device will maintain the means of communication (analog to analog, digital to digital, IP to IP) then the connection to that device would be called a trunk. If the expectation that the target device would change the means of communication (analog to PRI, IP to PRI, IP to analog, etc.) then the connection to that device would be a gateway.
Remember that the original PBXs were analog hosting analog phones. The connection between two PBXs would be a Trunk because it is a connection carrying one call between the two systems, maintaining the analog means of communication on both the source system and the target system. Even if the trunk connection between the two PBXs was a T1 or E1, it would be a channelized T1 or E1 with analog channels. So, again, like-to-like and therefore still a trunk.
For a connection from an analog PBX to a PSTN-connected device, the PSTN-connected device would be assumed to have a PRI and so that PSTN-connected device (and its connection) would be considered a gateway. (Analog to PRI)
As telephony devices and systems involved, the underlying concept was maintained that a like-to-like expectation would have the connection be a trunk, and a like-to-different expectation would have the connection be a gateway.
The earliest widely used IP telephony protocol was H.323, and that was used to connect the analog PBX to a larger IP-based network, so an H.323 connection was considered a gateway. Once the technology existed such that H.323-based endpoints (especially early video) connected to H.323-based routers which reached out to other H.323 routers to eventually get a call to another H.323 video device, the term "gateway" for those initial connections stuck around, but you did hear the term "Trunk" for the inter-router H.323 connections.
This means that MGCP would be a gateway because it is IP phones on CUCM to an MGCP-managed PRI. For CUCM an H.323 connection was assumed to be a connection to a router-controlled PRI, so that was also a gateway.
If you think about CUCM intercluster trunks (the old kind, not SIP trunks) the expectation is that the source system and the target system would maintain the IP-based means of communication, so those are trunks.
SIP is modern IP-based communications and the expectation is that the target device will also be IP-based (and usually SIP) so that connection was named a trunk from the start. That is true whether it is a SIP intercluster trunk between two CUCM clusters, or a SIP trunk to an external system. SIP assumes IP-only even if the target does 'something else' on the other end, so SIP connections are all trunks.
Generically, a gateway is a device that connects systems that are not alike. Back in the days when token ring and Ethernet were interworked, the device connecting them was called a translational gateway. And if you think about the networking term "ip default gateway" you are connecting "inside" to "outside" and the term gateway makes sense.
To answer the original poster's question, as [@Jonathan Schulenberg](https://community.cisco.com/t5/user/viewprofilepage/user-id/324968) pointed out it depends on what you want to do. If you want CUCM to control the router and the FXO ports, then you would use MGCP. If you want CUCM to connect to the router, but have the router control the FXO ports then CUCM could either use an H.323 connection to the router or a SIP connection to the router. But SIP is recommended as it is more modern, while H.323 is being phased out of support, so you want to use SIP.
I hope you find this helpful. I know it was long-winded, but I find understanding the history behind a thing helps me remember it better and the context helps me apply it to new situations. Please let me know if you have any questions or if I can help with other information.

A Voice Gateway and a SIP Trunk are related, but they solve different problems.
Think of it like this: A SIP Trunk is the road that carries phone calls over the internet. A Voice Gateway is the bridge/interpreter that connects old phone systems to that road.
### SIP Trunk
A SIP Trunk replaces traditional phone lines.  
It connects your PBX, IP phone system, or cloud calling platform directly to a telecom provider using the internet.
You use a SIP Trunk when: Your phone system already supports VoIP/SIP, You want cheaper calling costs, You need scalable phone lines, and, You are moving from analog/PRI to modern cloud telephony
Example:  
Your company uses an IP-PBX like Asterisk, FreePBX, or Cisco CUCM, and you want external calling through an internet provider — you use a SIP Trunk.
### Voice Gateway
A Voice Gateway is hardware (or sometimes software) that converts: Analog ↔ Digital, PSTN ↔ VoIP, and Legacy telecom protocols ↔ SIP
It helps older systems communicate with modern VoIP networks.
You use a Voice Gateway when:
  * You still have analog phones or fax machines
  * Your PBX only supports PRI/E1/FXO/FXS
  * You need interoperability between old and new systems
  * You want gradual migration to VoIP instead of replacing everything


Example:  
A company has an old Panasonic PBX with PRI lines but wants to connect to a SIP provider.  
A Voice Gateway sits in between and translates everything.
### Simple Decision Rule
  * Use SIP Trunk only → when your infrastructure is already IP-based.
  * Use Voice Gateway + SIP Trunk → when legacy telecom equipment still exists.


### In practical deployments
Many enterprises actually use both:
  1. SIP Trunk for carrier connectivity
  2. Voice Gateway for compatibility, survivability, or branch office integration


A wise way to think about it is: “A trunk gives you connectivity.
A gateway gives you compatibility.”
One moves calls.  
The other makes different worlds understand each other.
